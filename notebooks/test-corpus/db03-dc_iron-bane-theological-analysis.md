# Iron-Bane Theological Analysis — Svin-fylking as Cursed Beast

## Overview

This documentation examines Iron-Bane Crusade theological doctrine regarding the Svin-fylking—the creatures most commonly known as Boar Moons. Where other factions see dangerous beasts or economic opportunity, the Iron-Bane Crusade sees victims of the Iron-Curse: innocent animals made monstrous by corruption seeping from the earth.

The analysis draws primarily from *The Litany of the Three Curses* and Anvil-Cant doctrinal teachings, supplemented by field observation of Iron-Bane hunting parties and interviews with Crusade theologians.

---

## Doctrinal Position

### The Iron-Curse Manifestation

Iron-Bane theology classifies Svin-fylking as "Flesh-Iron Abominations"—ranking them below the Undying and Iron-Husks but above mundane beasts in the hierarchy of corrupted things. The doctrine holds that these creatures were once ordinary animals, twisted by the Iron-Curse that seeps from buried pre-Glitch contamination.

The evidence cited for this interpretation includes the Iron Husk formations that sometimes develop on elder specimens, the unnatural tactical coordination observed in warband behavior, and the creatures' apparent targeting of human settlements—behavior seen as too purposeful for mere beasts.

> *"The Iron-Curse spreads through soil and water, corrupting innocent flesh. The beast that drinks from tainted streams becomes a vessel for the Static-Curse. It is not evil—it is enslaved."*

The moral status assigned is that of victims rather than inherent evil. But the corruption is considered irredeemable. Once touched by the Iron-Curse, a beast cannot be saved—only freed through death.

## Ritual Practices

### Hunt-as-Mercy

Iron-Bane doctrine frames the hunting of Svin-fylking as an act of compassion rather than aggression. The Crusaders do not hunt for sport or profit, but to free corrupted creatures from their "iron prison."

Ritual preparation for such hunts involves fasting and purification rites extending several days. Weapons are consecrated before use. The kill itself should be swift—mercy, not prolonged suffering. After the kill, a blessing is spoken over the corpse, and any iron present in the remains must be burned or buried rather than salvaged.

### Iron Husk Destruction

The Iron Husk formations found on some specimens present particular theological concern. Iron-Bane doctrine absolutely prohibits this material from entering human use. It must be destroyed through forge-fire or acid dissolution.

Crusader patrols monitor Rust-Clan markets and confiscate contraband Iron Husk material when found. Penalties for trading in such materials include fines, confiscation, and temporary exile for repeat offenders.

The one exception involves bone and tusk material, which may be salvaged after proper consecration rites—though even this practice faces criticism from purist factions within the Crusade.

## Factional Tensions

### Conflict with Rust-Clan Economics

The most significant friction arises with the Rust-Clan, whose economy depends partly on Iron Husk salvage. The Iron-Bane demand for destruction directly conflicts with Rust-Clan profit interests.

An uneasy tolerance exists in remote areas, while strict enforcement occurs near Crusade strongholds. Iron-Bane presence in a region notably depresses salvage prices, as traders factor in confiscation risk. Violent clashes over salvage rights have been recorded, though both sides generally prefer to avoid escalation.

### Theological Conflict with God-Sleepers

God-Sleeper doctrine views Iron Husk material as a gift from the Forge-Gods—a position Iron-Banes consider heresy. The theological debate continues unresolved, with no shared authority capable of adjudicating between such fundamentally opposed positions.

Practical conflict remains minimal, as the two groups rarely occupy the same territories.

### Common Ground with Dvergr

Surprisingly, Iron-Banes and Dvergr find limited common ground regarding Iron Husk material. Both consider it problematic—though for different reasons. The Dvergr see it as contaminated and economically marginal; the Iron-Banes see spiritual corruption.

This shared distaste enables limited trade agreements: Dvergr will accept consecrated bone and tusk from Iron-Bane sources, recognizing the purification rites even if they do not share the theology behind them.

## Sermons and Parables

### The Parable of the Iron Boar

A cautionary tale tells of a farmer who fed iron scraps to pigs rather than disposing of them properly. The pigs transformed into monsters and slew their owner. The moral: the Iron-Curse spreads through negligence and greed. The application: warning against careless disposal of iron near livestock or wildlife.

### The Litany of the Three Irons

> *"Three irons we quench: the Heart that hums, the Husk that walks, the Word that stains."*

This litany names the three categories of Iron-Curse manifestation: the Undying (humming hearts), the Svin-fylking (walking husks), and corrupted runes (staining words). Crusaders chant it before hunts, forge-work, and exorcisms.

## Internal Debates

The Crusade contains factions with differing interpretations of proper doctrine.

**Mercy versus Extermination:** The dominant position holds that Svin-fylking should be killed only when necessary, with death granted swiftly. A fringe extermination faction argues for proactive elimination of all corrupted beasts as prevention.

**Salvage Pragmatism:** Debate continues over whether bone and tusk salvage—even after consecration—undermines the purification doctrine. Purists argue that any use of cursed material spreads corruption. Pragmatists note economic necessity. Current practice permits salvage after proper rites, though the theological tension persists.

---

## Strategic Implications

The Crusade commits meaningful military resources to warband tracking and elimination. Successful hunts serve multiple purposes: defending threatened settlements generates goodwill, demonstrates Crusade effectiveness, and supports recruitment efforts.

The theological framing transforms what others see as pest control into sacred duty—giving Crusaders religious motivation beyond mere survival or profit.
