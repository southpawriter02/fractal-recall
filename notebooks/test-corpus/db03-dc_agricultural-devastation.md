# Agricultural Devastation Case Study - Wheatholm District Total Loss, Year 781-783 PG

**Document ID:** DC-MYC-AGRI-001
**Classification:** Socioeconomic Impact Assessment / Longitudinal Study
**Location:** Midgard, Wheatholm District (Agricultural Zone 7)
**Study Period:** Pre-emergence (Year 780 PG) through post-emergence (Year 783 PG)
**Lead Researcher:** Farmer's Guild Liaison, Steward Aldric Fieldson
**Contributing Authors:** Midgard Combine Agricultural Council; Scriptorium-Midgard Economics Division
**Purpose:** Document total transformation of productive agricultural land into Mycelian Weaver contamination zone

---

## Pre-Emergence Baseline: Wheatholm District Profile

### Geographic and Economic Overview

Wheatholm District lies in the Midgard interior, several days' travel northeast of Combine Central. The district comprised extensive productive farmland—among the more fertile zones in the region—with wheat as the primary crop alongside barley, rye, and vegetables. The annual yield represented a meaningful portion of regional grain supply, supporting over a hundred residents across more than twenty farming families.

The district had been established generations prior, with third-generation farming families working land renowned for its fertility. Later analysis suggested the rich soil owed its quality to buried pre-Glitch infrastructure providing metal-rich substrate—a detail that would prove tragically relevant.

### Pre-Emergence Warnings (Unrecognized)

In the autumn preceding emergence, farmers reported unusual signs: fungal growth in the north fields, livestock refusing to graze specific areas, slight ground subsidence dismissed as normal settling, and increased insect die-off. A Ranger's Guild survey that winter noted "minor mycelial presence" in subsurface scans but assessed the risk as low priority. No evacuation was recommended.

*Hindsight suggests early warning signs were present but not recognized as tower emergence precursors. Pattern recognition has improved in subsequent years.*

---

## Tower Emergence Event: Spring 781 PG

### Emergence Timeline

On the Spring Equinox of Year 781 PG, multiple families reported ground tremors and deep rumbling before dawn. By morning, a large bulge had appeared in the north field. Witnesses described watching it grow "like bread rising." By midday, the tower had broken the surface and reached several stories in height. By evening, the first spore release was observed.

Over the following days, the tower continued its rapid ascent. Fungal constructs emerged and began patrolling the perimeter. The north field families evacuated voluntarily on the second day. By the third day, the Ranger's Guild had established a quarantine perimeter and issued mandatory evacuation orders. Within a week, all families had been evacuated and the district was declared an agricultural loss zone.

### Initial Response

**Testimonial from Goodwife Mara Seedkeeper:**

> "We'd just planted the spring wheat. Three generations of our family have worked this land. My grandfather cleared the stones, my father built the barn, my husband and I raised our children here.
>
> Then this... *thing*... erupted from our north field. In one night. One night! Everything we'd built, everything we'd worked for, just... gone. Consumed.
>
> They made us leave. The Rangers said the air was poison, that the spores would kill us. My husband wanted to stay, to fight it somehow. I had to drag him away.
>
> We left everything. Tools, furniture, winter stores. Left it all to be eaten by that *thing*.
>
> You want to know the worst part? When we looked back from the evacuation wagons, we could see it *growing*. Taller every hour. Like it was mocking us.
>
> Where do we go now? Who do we become, if we're not the people who work the Wheatholm land?"

## Progressive Contamination: The Following Months

### Tower Growth and Stabilization

During the first month, the tower continued its vertical growth before beginning to plateau. Over subsequent months, vertical growth slowed while horizontal expansion accelerated. The tower eventually stabilized at a height comparable to the tallest structures in Combine Central.

### Contamination Radius Expansion

The contamination spread outward in concentric zones. The innermost zone—total die-off—expanded steadily over the months. Beyond this lay a mutation zone where surviving vegetation showed alarming changes. The outermost zone experienced chronic spore exposure affecting yields and livestock behavior.

By half a year after emergence, contamination had extended well beyond original Wheatholm boundaries into neighboring districts. The agricultural loss far exceeded initial projections.

### Infrastructure Consumption

All abandoned structures were eventually consumed by the mycelial network. Field observations documented the process: within weeks of abandonment, mycelial threads appeared on exterior walls, infiltrated structures, caused wooden components to decay, dissolved metal fittings, and eventually collapsed buildings into unrecognizable mounds fully integrated into the mycelial mat.

*"It's not destruction. It's digestion. The Weaver doesn't demolish structures—it slowly, patiently, dissolves them into component materials and absorbs them into its network. The Seedkeeper farm—generations of family history—became soil. Just soil."*

## Human Impact: Displacement and Despair

### Refugee Population

All district residents—over a hundred individuals across more than twenty families—were displaced. Immediate relocation was to a Combine refugee camp. Long-term outcomes varied: some families resettled in other agricultural zones on inferior land; others transitioned to urban labor, losing their farming lifestyle; some families fragmented as younger generations scattered; and a few joined Grove-Clan communities seeking refuge from what they called "cursed land."

None returned. Return was impossible; the land remained uninhabitable.

### Psychological Impact

Assessments conducted months after displacement revealed profound trauma. The majority reported symptoms of traumatic loss—"mourning the land." Many experienced sleep disruption with nightmares of the growing tower. Over half reported identity crisis: "if I'm not a Wheatholm farmer, who am I?" Many reported survivor's guilt and severe depression requiring treatment.

Common themes emerged from interviews: a sense of betrayal by land that "turned against us," generational grief over failing ancestors, loss of future for children who were supposed to inherit, and anger at the Scriptorium for failing to warn or stop the emergence.

### Case Study: The Thornfeld Family

The trajectory of the Thornfeld family illustrates the full human cost. Marcus, a fourth-generation farmer, evacuated with his wife Elara and three children. Granted resettlement land that proved rocky and less fertile, Marcus struggled with depression. Months later, unable to reconcile his identity with farming "someone else's inferior land," he required psychiatric care. The family eventually fragmented—divorce proceedings, children scattered, the family unit dissolved.

*"The tower didn't just take our land. It took our family. We couldn't survive the loss."* —Elara Thornfeld

## Regional Food Security Impact

### Supply Disruption

The loss of Wheatholm's substantial grain production created an immediate supply shock. Regional grain prices increased noticeably within months. As contamination spread into neighboring districts, additional productive land was lost, compounding the shortage.

### Combine Response

Short-term measures included releasing emergency grain reserves, implementing price controls, and establishing rationing protocols for urban populations. Long-term responses involved agricultural expansion into marginal lands, increased importation from distant regions at higher cost, development of "tower-resistant" farming strategies emphasizing mobility and rapid abandonment protocols, and investment in alternate food sources.

The grim reality remained: lost agricultural land was permanent. Productivity losses had to be absorbed through expansion elsewhere or accepted as permanent reduction.

## Failed Intervention Attempts

### Alchemical Eradication

An attempt was made using large-scale application of Blight-Cleanser Solvent to the tower base and immediate mycelial mat. Initial results appeared promising—visible surface necrosis suggested success. Within days, however, regrowth was evident. Within a week, the mycelial mat had fully regenerated; the application area became indistinguishable from untreated zones.

*Conclusion: Surface application proved insufficient. The deep mycelial network extending far underground remained unaffected. The tower regenerated faster than eradication could proceed. The method was abandoned as economically unsustainable.*

### Rúnasmiðr Containment

A runic firewall was attempted to halt spore dispersal, using an inverted Berkano script array with Pure Steel anchors and Nidavellir crystals. Initial results showed reduced spore dispersal within the warded zone. However, the ward required unexpectedly frequent recalibration. Subsurface mycelial infiltration compromised anchor points. The ward eventually failed as mycelial growth breached the perimeter in multiple locations.

*Conclusion: The mycelial network extended underground, beneath ward foundations. Surface-only wards proved ineffective against subsurface spread. The method was abandoned as impractical for large-scale containment.*

### Construct Elimination Campaign

A systematic campaign attempted to destroy fungal constructs, reasoning that eliminating the Weaver's "agents" would disrupt material collection. Rangers engaged constructs successfully over two weeks. However, construct regeneration exceeded elimination rate. The campaign had the opposite of its intended effect—eliminations appeared to stimulate increased production.

*Conclusion: The tower functions as a "construct factory" with regeneration capacity exceeding any sustainable elimination rate. The method was abandoned as counterproductive.*

## Lessons Learned

### Tower Emergence is Effectively Irreversible

Once a Blight-Fungus Tower emerges, eradication appears impossible with current capabilities. Containment proves impractical at necessary scales. Permanent territorial loss must be accepted.

**Strategic Implication:** Focus must shift from prevention (which appears impossible) to prediction and rapid evacuation.

### Early Warning Systems are Critical

Retrospective analysis identified indicators that preceded emergence: subsurface mycelial presence detectable via Dvergr acoustic survey, ground temperature elevation, livestock behavioral changes, vegetation die-off in circular patterns, and minor subsidence.

Quarterly subsurface surveys have since been implemented in high-risk zones. The approach has shown partial success—some evacuations have preempted tower impacts, though prediction remains imperfect.

### Agricultural Adaptation Required

The new reality demands agricultural systems capable of rapid abandonment: minimal permanent infrastructure, portable equipment, pre-planned evacuation routes, and psychological preparation for impermanence. Diversification across wider geographic areas reduces regional dependence on any single zone.

Most difficult is the emotional adaptation required. Farmers must accept that land is no longer permanent. Multi-generational attachment to specific fields has become liability rather than asset.

## Steward's Reflection

*From Steward Aldric Fieldson's closing remarks:*

> I grew up in farm country. My father was a farmer. His father before him. I understand, intimately, what the Wheatholm families lost.
>
> It's not just land. Land is abstract. What they lost was *their* land. The north field where Marcus proposed to Elara. The old oak where Mara's children played. The stone fence Grandfather built with his own hands.
>
> They lost *meaning*. They lost the story of who they are.
>
> And the cruelest part? The tower doesn't care. The Root-Mind doesn't even perceive their grief. To the Weaver, Wheatholm is just... more material to digest. More organic matter to recycle.
>
> I've been asked: "How do we fight this?"
>
> The answer is: *We don't*. We can't. The Weaver operates on a timescale that makes human resistance meaningless. We can measure it, document it, warn each other. But we cannot stop it.
>
> What we can do—what we *must* do—is adapt. Accept that land is temporary. Accept that the patient earth is reclaiming what we built. Accept that farming, in this new age, means knowing when to let go and move on.
>
> The Weaver is not our enemy. It is not an enemy at all.
>
> It is the future. The slow, quiet, inevitable future.
>
> And we must learn to live in its shadow.

*— Steward Aldric Fieldson, Year 782 PG*
